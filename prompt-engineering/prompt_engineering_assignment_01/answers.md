### Water Bottle, Version 4: Constraints and output format

**Prompt**

> Describe how water bottles work and why there are so many different brands when they seemingly do the same thing. Keep it concise with a serious tone. The reading level should be around high school to college level, and have the facts in a numbered list.

**Output**

1. **How Water Bottles Work:** Water bottles are containers designed to store and transport liquids while preventing spills and contamination. They use a sealed lid or cap to keep liquid contained and allow controlled access when drinking.

2. **Material Differences:** Water bottles are manufactured using materials such as plastic, stainless steel, glass, and aluminum. Each material offers different advantages in terms of durability, weight, insulation, cost, and environmental impact.

3. **Insulation Technology:** Some bottles use double-wall vacuum insulation to reduce heat transfer, keeping beverages hot or cold for extended periods. Others use single-wall construction, prioritizing affordability and lightweight design.

4. **Design and Functionality:** Brands differentiate their products through features such as built-in straws, leak-proof lids, ergonomic shapes, carrying handles, and adjustable drinking mechanisms. These features target different consumer needs and preferences.

5. **Brand Competition:** Although most water bottles serve the same primary purpose, brands compete through product quality, appearance, pricing, innovation, and reputation. Marketing and brand recognition also influence consumer purchasing decisions.

6. **Consumer Preferences:** The large number of water bottle brands exists because consumers value different characteristics. Some prioritize affordability, while others prefer durability, temperature retention, convenience, sustainability, or aesthetics.

7. **Market Differentiation:** Ultimately, water bottle companies compete by improving the experience surrounding a relatively simple product. Small differences in design, materials, and functionality allow brands to target specific audiences and establish a competitive advantage.

## 2. Analysis

**Effect of each technique**

- **Few-shot (Water Bottle V1 to V2): improved.** V1 was generic, with no product name and features that fit any bottle. V2 invented features that stand out: a hydration tracker and a self-cleaning UV-C lid. It also copied the examples' one-sentence format, so the output got much shorter.
- **Role and context (V2 to V3): mixed.** The tone became persuasive: a named product (HydroFlow), headings and a tagline. But the unique V2 features were lost and replaced with standard ones like insulation and a leak-resistant lid.
- **Constraints and output format (V3 to V4): improved.** The model followed the numbered list, serious tone and reading level, and stuck to facts instead of marketing. It was still a bit long at 7 points despite "concise."
- **Context and constraints (Standing Desk): mostly improved.** It kept to 3 options with headlines and bullets, but added a table and a recommendation I didn't ask for.
- **All four techniques combined (Phone Case): format improved, accuracy didn't.** It matched my Pros/Cons layout exactly, stayed under $40 and rated durability for each case.

**Ignored instructions and made-up details**

- It said warranty was a factor but listed no warranties.
- It ignored "keep it concise" for the phone cases, giving 5 options plus a table and tips.
- The desk table had specs not in the descriptions: OffiGo's 154 lb capacity and 3 memory presets.
- It named the first desk two ways: "Joyful" and "Inbox Zero."

**Fixes for next time:** set a hard limit on the number of options, allow only specs that are in the listing, and carry key features forward into the next prompt.

Few-shot prompting made the biggest difference. It gave the most direct control over format and length, as in Water Bottle V2 and the phone case. Examples show the model what you want instead of describing it, so it guesses less. The downside is that it copies everything, including a length you didn't intend.
